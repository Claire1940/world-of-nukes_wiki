<Section icon={Shield} title="What Defines a Nuclear Power?">

A nuclear power is any state that owns nuclear weapons and maintains the infrastructure to build, maintain, and deliver them. The term usually carries both a technical and a political meaning: technically, it describes capability; politically, it separates the recognized "haves" from the "have-nots" under international law.

Only five nations are officially recognized as nuclear-weapon states under the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT): the **United States**, **Russia**, **China**, **France**, and the **United Kingdom**. Four additional states possess nuclear weapons outside the treaty framework, which makes the global picture more complicated than the legal one.

<Callout type="tip" title="Recognition Matters">
Nuclear weapons capability alone does not grant formal status. India, Pakistan, Israel, and North Korea all tested or acquired weapons outside the NPT, and none enjoys the treaty privileges granted to the five recognized powers.
</Callout>

The nine nuclear powers differ enormously in arsenal size, delivery systems, doctrine, and transparency. The table below gives a quick comparison of their key characteristics.

| Country | Status | First Test | Noted Delivery Systems |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | NPT recognized | 1945 | ICBMs, SLBMs, strategic bombers |
| Russia | NPT recognized | 1949 | ICBMs, SLBMs, hypersonic systems |
| United Kingdom | NPT recognized | 1952 | Submarine-launched missiles |
| France | NPT recognized | 1960 | Air-launched and sea-launched missiles |
| China | NPT recognized | 1964 | ICBMs, SLBMs, bomber fleet |
| India | Outside NPT | 1974 | Land-based missiles, aircraft |
| Pakistan | Outside NPT | 1998 | Land-based missiles, aircraft |
| Israel | Undeclared | N/A | Widely assessed as nuclear-capable |
| North Korea | Withdrew from NPT | 2006 | Short-range and developmental missiles |

<Section icon={Map} title="From Trinity to Today: How the Nuclear Club Formed">

The nuclear age began in July 1945 with the Trinity test in New Mexico, followed by the wartime bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Within four years the Soviet Union tested its own device, and the arms race accelerated through the 1950s as Britain, France, and China joined the club in quick succession.

<Callout type="warning" title="The Cold War Peak">
At its height in the mid-1980s, the global stockpile reached tens of thousands of warheads. The overwhelming majority belonged to the United States and the Soviet Union, whose rivalry drove both buildup and eventual arms control.
</Callout>

A lesser-known chapter involves former Soviet republics. When the USSR dissolved in 1991, Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan inherited nuclear weapons on their territory. All three transferred them to Russia and joined the NPT as non-nuclear states. Kazakhstan's decision carries special weight: the Semipalatinsk test site had exposed its population to decades of radiation, making the country one of the places most affected by nuclear testing anywhere in the world.

That legacy keeps Kazakhstan active in disarmament diplomacy. International physicians' groups have convened in Astana to press world powers to eliminate nuclear weapons, drawing directly on the country's experience as a living reminder of testing's human cost.

<Section icon={Target} title="Treaties, Doctrines, and Arms Control">

The legal architecture around nuclear weapons rests on several layered agreements. The NPT (1970) is the foundation: recognized nuclear states pledge eventual disarmament, while everyone else pledges not to acquire weapons. Supplementary treaties constrain testing, deployment, and warhead counts.

| Treaty | Year | Core Purpose | Current Relevance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NPT | 1968/1970 | Prevent spread, promote disarmament | Near-universal membership |
| CTBT | 1996 | Ban all nuclear explosions | Not fully in force; testing moratorium mostly holds |
| START / New START | 2010 | Cap US-Russia deployed warheads | Central bilateral limitation framework |
| TPNW | 2017 | Ban nuclear weapons outright | Nuclear states have not joined |
| NWFZ treaties | 1967+ | Regional nuclear-weapon-free zones | Cover Latin America, Africa, Central Asia, Pacific |

<Callout type="info" title="Doctrine Shapes Risk">
Arsenal size is only half the story. Whether a state pledges "no first use," keeps weapons on high alert, or treats them as war-fighting tools determines how dangerous those weapons are in a crisis.
</Callout>

Understanding a nuclear power's doctrine helps explain its arsenal choices:

- **Assured destruction**: maintaining a retaliatory force that survives any attack.
- **No first use**: a declared pledge to use nuclear weapons only in response to a nuclear attack.
- **Extended deterrence**: promising to defend allies with nuclear weapons, as the United States does through NATO and Pacific alliances.
- **Minimum deterrence**: keeping a small arsenal sufficient only for retaliation, as several smaller nuclear states practice.

</Section>

<Section icon={Sparkles} title="How to Research Nuclear Powers Responsibly">

Follow this workflow to build an accurate, source-grounded picture of the world's nuclear arsenals without falling for speculation or propaganda.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Start With Official Sources">
    Check the IAEA, UN Office for Disarmament Affairs, and national defense ministry publications first. These provide treaty texts, declarations, and inspection frameworks that anchor everything else.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Cross-Reference Independent Estimates">
    Compare figures from respected independent trackers such as the Federation of American Scientists and SIPRI. Exact warhead counts are estimates for most states, so ranges matter more than single numbers.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Separate Capability From Intent">
    Distinguish verified tests and deployed systems from claims, rumors, and developmental programs. Undeclared programs require multiple corroborating sources before treating them as fact.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Track Treaty Milestones">
    Follow NPT review cycles, New START deadlines, and TPNW meetings. Legal developments often reshape the strategic landscape faster than new weapons do.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Study the Human Legacy">
    Read survivor testimony and health research from Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Semipalatinsk, and Pacific test sites. This context explains why disarmament advocacy remains persistent and credible.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="success" title="Best Practice">
Treat every warhead count as an estimate with a stated range and a named source. Precise-sounding numbers without attribution are the most common sign of unreliable nuclear reporting.
</Callout>

</Section>
