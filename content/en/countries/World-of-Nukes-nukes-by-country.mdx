<Section icon={Map} title="Nukes by Country: The Nine Nuclear-Armed States">

Understanding **nukes by country** starts with a single date: July 16, 1945, when US scientists detonated the world's first nuclear device in the New Mexico desert. Three weeks later, atomic bombs fell on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, killing more than 200,000 people and closing World War II. Eighty years on, nine countries possess nuclear weapons, and researchers estimate the global stockpile at more than 12,000 warheads — including active, stored, and retired units.

The nuclear club divides into three tiers: the five treaty-recognized powers, the three non-signatories, and the one state that joined through covert proliferation networks.

### Israel: Strategic Ambiguity

Israel has never confirmed or denied possessing nuclear weapons. In the late 1950s, with French help, it began work on a secret facility at Dimona, publicly described as nuclear energy research. The world learned the truth largely through whistleblower Mordechai Vanunu, a former Dimona technician who leaked details to a British newspaper in 1986 and spent 18 years in an Israeli prison. This policy of ambiguity lets Israel gain deterrence benefits while avoiding the full diplomatic consequences faced by other unsanctioned nuclear regimes.

### India and Pakistan: A Regional Arms Race

India's program benefited from the US-led Atoms for Peace initiative — America supplied nuclear materials, and Canada provided a reactor. After a 1962 war with China, India secretly enriched plutonium and tested a device in 1974, calling it a "peaceful nuclear explosion." Pakistan, having fought India just three years earlier, resolved to match it. Physicist Abdul Qadeer Khan smuggled enrichment designs from the Netherlands, and China added technical knowledge in the late 1980s. In May 1998, both nations conducted overt test sequences, transforming South Asia into a nuclear-armed region.

### North Korea: Fifty Years to a Bomb

North Korea's nuclear path began in the 1950s with Soviet civilian assistance, but a dedicated weapons program likely did not emerge until the 1970s. Khan's proliferation network supplied key designs. Although originally an NPT signatory, North Korea withdrew in 2003, expelled inspectors, and tested its first device in 2006 — roughly half a century after starting.

<Callout type="warning" title="The AQ Khan Network">
Abdul Qadeer Khan did not stop at Pakistan. He **sold nuclear weapons knowledge to other countries, including North Korea** — making his network one of the most consequential proliferation channels in nuclear history.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Target} title="The NPT Framework: Who Is Allowed Nukes">

The Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), drawn up amid 1960s fears of endless nuclear expansion, created a simple dividing line: any nation that had tested a nuclear weapon by 1967 could keep them as "nuclear weapon states," while all other signatories could not. The treaty's central bargain requires those five states to work in good faith toward disarmament.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="1945–1962: The Arms Race Ignites">
    The US bombs Japan; the USSR, UK, France, and China follow. Fusion bombs, exponentially more powerful than the 1945 fission designs, dominate development from the early 1950s.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="1953: Atoms for Peace">
    Eisenhower's UN speech launches an initiative steering nuclear technology toward energy and research, leading to the founding of the IAEA — the UN's nuclear watchdog.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="1968: The NPT Draws the Line">
    The treaty freezes legal nuclear status at five states and obligates them to pursue disarmament. Most of the world signs.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="1990s: Post-Soviet Rollback">
    Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan inherit Soviet arms, then sign the NPT and give them up. South Africa — never a signatory — secretly built six-style devices in the 1970s–80s and dismantled them as apartheid ended.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="1998–2006: The Treaty Is Tested">
    India and Pakistan test openly; North Korea withdraws and detonates its first device, becoming the ninth nuclear state.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="info" title="Why South Africa Matters">
South Africa remains the **significant outlier of the nuclear age** — the only state to build its own nuclear weapons and then voluntarily dismantle them before joining the NPT.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section icon={Sparkles} title="The Iran Question and Modern Proliferation Risks">

Iran represents the most watched nuclear file in the world today. It received a US reactor in 1967 under Atoms for Peace and signed the NPT a year later — meaning it is legally barred from developing weapons. Yet its history is marked by concealment:

| Year | Event | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2002 | Secret sites at Arak and Natanz exposed | Enrichment plant built partly on **AQ Khan designs** |
| 2009 | Fordow facility imaged by satellites | Configuration "inconsistent with a peaceful programme" |
| June 2025 | IAEA cites NPT breaches | Iran found in breach of treaty obligations |
| June 2025 | Israel strikes, US follows | Strikes on enrichment infrastructure |
| Aftermath | Iran suspends IAEA cooperation | Inspectors shut out of verification work |

Iran has always maintained it never sought a bomb, and experts assess it likely has not made the decision to build one — though technically and technologically, there is no barrier preventing it. If Iran crossed the threshold, analysts warn of a **domino effect** in the Middle East, where neighboring states might pursue their own capabilities.

The NPT's two founding principles — halting spread beyond the five, and disarmament — are both under strain. Nuclear states point to enormous stockpile reductions since the Cold War peak; non-nuclear states point out that all five recognized powers are modernizing and, in some cases, expanding their arsenals.

<Callout type="warning" title="The Verification Gap">
Israel, India, and Pakistan — never having signed the NPT — leave UN inspectors **without legal power to examine their nuclear facilities**. After the 2025 strikes, Iran joined them in shutting out the IAEA, weakening global verification.
</Callout>
